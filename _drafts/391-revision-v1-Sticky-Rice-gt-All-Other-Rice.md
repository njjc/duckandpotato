Normally, I'm not really a carbs girl unless they're sweet. You offer me bread, I might wait and see what else there is before I commit. You offer me cake, there's no cake because I already ate it. The exception to that rule is rice. Specifically, Thai sticky rice. Sticky rice has a quality of chew that is sublime- somehow being tender and chewy simultaneously. It's also beautiful, like little translucent pearls. And: it tastes toasty and lightly fragrant, not perfume-y fragrant like jasmine rice (not actually jasmine flavored, you understand, but known to be fragrant and floral LIKE jasmine). I like the way sticky rice insists on being an equal partner in a meal, too. With regular brown, white or jasmine rice (arborio, I exclude you from this) it's just a filler, something you put the thing you're actually eating on top of. The way sticky rice is eaten in northern Thailand means it demands that you consider it for itself. Before you take a bite of something, you grab a hunk of rice and smoosh it into a little ball with one hand, making a little divot in it with your thumb like an edible spoon made out of rice, all the while considering which delicious thing you should dip it into. I hadn't made sticky rice at home until recently, when Nic and I were at our local excellent Asian grocery store, Fubonn. I wanted to buy one of the reed steamer baskets like they use in Thailand, but Nic talked me out of it, citing both Alton Brown's distaste for uni-tasking kitchen gadgets AND the perfectly good steamer our realtor gave us as a gift when we bought our house. He noted that I probably forgot we had a steamer because I hate steamed food. Point Nic. You need two pieces of equipment to make sticky rice. You need a steamer, or some kind of steaming setup (like a big pot with a fitted lid and then a colander or strainer that can sit in the pot above the water to put the rice in while the water boils below) and you need [this type of nylon mesh bag](https://www.amazon.com/K7-0SHH-5IIE-Premium-Food-Grade-Almond/dp/B00158U8DU/ref=sr_1_9?ie=UTF8&qid=1485970280&sr=8-9&keywords=brewing+bag). You can use the mesh bag for many things - making nut milks (the only thing funnier to me than the phrase "nut milks" is the fact that they call shelled pistachios "nutmeats"), brewing beer, making sticky rice, probably other things if you ever steam food other than sticky rice, which I don't. You could MacGyver something else to use instead of the nylon mesh bag, like cheesecloth or something, but I would encourage you to fork out the $6.88 for the bag. It makes the process really easy. The other thing you need in addition to the equipment is the sticky rice itself. You can buy sticky rice at any Asian grocery store\*. The bag might say "sweet rice" or "glutinous rice" or "sticky rice" or even all three. It should definitely be a product of Thailand. Once you have those three items, you're ready to enjoy the finest rice this earth has to offer. \*You visit your local Asian grocery store regularly, right? If not, you're missing out. The cheap produce, interesting meats (my neighborhood store doesn't ever stock pork belly or duck legs, is all I'm saying) and frozen dumplings are reason enough for a visit, but venture down the aisle of fermented bean pastes and you will be forever changed. Also, my particular Asian grocery store has all the weird flavors of Ritter Sport at the checkout. Did you know they make a Ritter Sport that is milk chocolate and TORTILLA CHIPS? Well they do, and it's delicious. PIC OF RICE BAG PIC OF STICKY RICE NEXT TO SUSHI RICE **Sticky Rice** **Ingredients/equipment:** Sweet rice, sticky rice or glutinous rice from Thailand Steamer setup of some kind Nylon mesh bag Water **Method:** In the morning before you go to work the day you want to eat sticky rice, put as much of the rice as you want to eat in a container with a spout (I usually cook 2 cups of dry rice at a time, and it is easy to reheat to perfection if there are leftovers, instructions on that to come). Cover the rice with cold, filtered water and mix it around a little with a spoon until the water turns white and cloudy, which it will do immediately. PIC of cloudy rice Pour out all that cloudy water without losing any of the rice (you can hold a spoon over the spout while you pour out the water to help with this). Do that same process again 5-6 times. Put new cold filtered water on the rice, stir it around until the water is cloudy, and then pour it out. The point of all this rinsing is to get some of the starch out of the rice. The starch is what turns the water cloudy and white. Rinsing some of it off is what makes the sticky rice sticky but still maintain individual rice grains and not become a glob of spackle. Soaking it in water all day gives the rice a headstart on cooking (just like soaking dry beans). There isn't really a shortcut to making sticky rice that doesn't involve soaking it for at least a few hours. PIC of finished rice When you've done that 5-6 times and the water is pretty clear but maybe still a little cloudy, cover the rice in enough new water to cover it by several inches and let it sit all day long on your counter until that night when you want to cook it. Most Thai recipes say to soak the rice overnight, but I've done it both overnight and in the morning and either is fine, so do whatever works for you. I'd say a solid 6-8 hours of soaking is good. When you're ready to cook the rice, about 30 minutes before you want to eat it, get your steamer setup boiling on the stove. Put enough water in your steamer bottom to get a roiling boil going (a few inches up the sides), and there should be a few inches of space between the top of the boiling water and the bottom of whatever you're putting your nylon bag of rice in - either the steamer basket if you're using a regular steamer, or your colander setup in the pot. The basket should not touch the water. We are steaming this sticky rice, not boiling it to death for crying out loud. When the water is a-boiling, pour the water off your rice and transfer the rice into the nylon mesh bag and cinch the drawstring at the top. Put the nylon bag in the steamer basket, and sort of flatten the bag of rice into the basket so it's more or less in an even layer. PIC of bag of rice in steamer Put the top on your pot, turn the heat down to maintain a steady but not angry boil and set a timer for 8 minutes. After 8 minutes, flip the bag of sticky rice over onto the other side, and boil for another 8 minutes. At that point, the sticky rice is done! If you've fudged your timing a bit and the other parts of your dinner aren't ready yet, sticky rice is pretty forgiving and will wait for you to get it together and won't turn into a mushy clump. Take the rice out of the steamer, dump it from the bag into a bowl, cover the bowl lightly with a lid, and that sticky rice will be ready basically whenever you are. PIC of finished rice To reheat leftover sticky rice, put it in a bowl, cover with a damp paper towel and microwave it a few times at 30-45 second intervals until it's heated all the way through. You'll know it's ready because it will regain the texture of sticky rice when it's hot enough and not feel like clumps of hardened mud anymore like it did when you took it out of the fridge.
